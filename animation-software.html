One creative goal.
Many possible tools.

Professional animation is rarely produced in a single application. A project may move between drawing, design, modelling, rigging, animation, simulation, lighting, rendering, compositing, sound and editing tools.

The right software is not automatically the most expensive or the most widely recognised. It is the tool—or combination of tools—that supports the project's style, the creator's skills, available hardware, budget, collaboration needs and final delivery format.

This guide introduces widely used examples. It is an educational overview, not a ranking or an endorsement. Product features, supported systems, licensing and prices can change; consult each vendor's current documentation before making a purchase or building a production pipeline.

01 / DRAWN & DIGITAL 2D

2D animation software.

2D tools support hand-drawn frame-by-frame animation, digital cut-out characters, vector animation and hybrid workflows. Some focus on the drawing experience; others combine drawing, rigging, compositing and production management features.

Harmony combines drawing, traditional paperless animation, character rigging, compositing and effects in a single production environment. Its different editions target a range of users, from enthusiasts to professional studios.

02 / THREE-DIMENSIONAL WORLDS

3D animation software.

3D production tools support the creation of digital objects, characters and environments, then help artists rig, animate, simulate, light and render those assets into finished images.

Maya is a broad 3D creation application used for modelling, rigging, character animation, simulation and effects. It is often encountered in professional film, television and games pipelines, where its extensibility and integration matter.

04 / HOW PROFESSIONAL PIPELINES WORK

What do major animation studios use?

Large studios may use commercial applications, proprietary tools, open standards and custom software developed around their own production requirements. A studio's pipeline is usually more complex than a list of off-the-shelf applications.

A DOCUMENTED EXAMPLE

Pixar and its technology ecosystem

Pixar publicly describes a technology organisation that develops tools and systems tailored to its filmmaking pipeline. Its published technology information highlights RenderMan, its rendering technology, and OpenUSD, an open-source framework for exchanging and collaborating on complex 3D scene data.

Pixar has also documented its proprietary Presto animation system and its development of production technology. This illustrates an important point: major studios may combine commercial software with internally developed applications and pipeline infrastructure.

Important distinction: A tool being used by a studio does not mean every production at that studio uses the same software. Production requirements, teams, projects and proprietary pipeline components vary. Avoid assuming that a single application defines an entire studio's workflow.
05 / MAKE AN INFORMED CHOICE

How to choose the right animation software.

Start with the work you want to make, then evaluate the tool against your production constraints. A sensible selection process prevents creators from paying for features they do not need or committing to a workflow that cannot deliver the project.

01

Define the output

Are you making hand-drawn cartoons, rigged 2D series, 3D films, motion graphics, game animation or short social videos?

02

Match the workflow

Check drawing, rigging, modelling, animation, rendering, compositing and collaboration features against your actual needs.

03

Check resources

Consider licensing cost, hardware, storage, internet access, training time and whether your team can support the software.

04

Test delivery

Run a small project and verify export formats, image quality, audio sync, frame rate and hand-off compatibility before scaling up.

06 / THE NETTOON CREATOR PERSPECTIVE

Great animation is not defined by one software package.

NETTOON's creator ecosystem should be open to animation made through different tools and production methods. A creator may use Blender, Toon Boom Harmony, Maya, Moho, OpenToonz, TVPaint or another suitable application. The important consideration is the quality, rights, technical integrity and delivery readiness of the finished work—not the brand of software used to create it.

As the platform develops, creator guidance can explain supported upload formats, encoding requirements, aspect ratios, frame rates, audio specifications and content delivery standards.
